Inheritance Tax in the UK – The Complete Independent Guide

Part 1: What inheritance tax is, who it applies to, and how the UK system works

Inheritance tax (IHT) is one of the most misunderstood taxes in the UK. Many people assume it only affects the very wealthy, or that it only applies to cash left behind after death.

In reality, inheritance tax often affects ordinary families, particularly where property values have increased over time. This guide explains how inheritance tax works in the UK, clearly and without pressure.

Introduction

Inheritance tax is charged on the value of a person’s estate when they die, and in some cases on gifts made during their lifetime.

The tax is calculated based on the total value of assets, less allowable exemptions and reliefs.

Key point

Inheritance tax is about value, timing, and structure — not just wealth.

What is inheritance tax?

Inheritance tax is a tax on the estate (property, money and possessions) of someone who has died. It can also apply to certain lifetime gifts.

The responsibility for dealing with inheritance tax usually falls on the executors or administrators of the estate.

Who pays inheritance tax?

Technically, inheritance tax is paid by the estate, not by the beneficiaries personally.

However, the impact is often felt by beneficiaries because tax reduces what is ultimately passed on.

Inheritance tax thresholds

Every individual has a tax-free threshold known as the nil-rate band.

There may also be an additional residence nil-rate band where a home is passed to direct descendants.

Inheritance tax rate

The standard rate of inheritance tax in the UK is 40% on the value of the estate above the available thresholds.

Lower rates may apply in limited circumstances, such as where part of the estate is left to charity.

What counts towards an estate?

  • Property and land
  • Money and savings
  • Investments
  • Business interests
  • Gifts made in certain circumstances

The legal framework

Inheritance tax is governed primarily by the Inheritance Tax Act 1984, alongside HMRC guidance and case law.

The rules are technical, and outcomes depend heavily on facts, timing and documentation.

Who is affected by inheritance tax in the UK?

One of the most persistent myths about inheritance tax is that it only affects the very wealthy. In reality, inheritance tax increasingly affects ordinary families, particularly where property values have risen.

You may be affected by inheritance tax if the total value of an estate — including property, savings, investments and certain gifts — exceeds available tax-free thresholds.

Key point

Inheritance tax is triggered by value, not income or lifestyle.

Common situations where inheritance tax arises

  • Owning property in the UK
  • Being single, divorced or widowed
  • Leaving assets to adult children or grandchildren
  • Having made gifts without surviving seven years
  • Owning business or agricultural assets without relief planning

The nil-rate band (basic threshold)

The nil-rate band is the amount an individual can pass on before inheritance tax becomes payable.

Anything above this threshold is potentially taxed at 40%, subject to reliefs and exemptions.

The nil-rate band applies per individual and does not automatically increase with asset values or inflation.

Key point

Many estates exceed the threshold simply due to property price growth.

The residence nil-rate band (RNRB)

In addition to the basic nil-rate band, an extra allowance — the residence nil-rate band — may apply where a qualifying home is passed to direct descendants.

Key conditions

  • The property must have been the deceased’s residence
  • It must be left to direct descendants (children, grandchildren)
  • The estate must not exceed taper thresholds

The residence nil-rate band is often misunderstood and can be lost entirely if conditions are not met.

Spouses and civil partners

Transfers between spouses or civil partners are generally exempt from inheritance tax.

This means that no inheritance tax is usually due on the first death, regardless of estate size.

Transferable allowances

Any unused nil-rate band and residence nil-rate band can normally be transferred to the surviving spouse’s estate.

Key point

Many estates rely heavily on transferred allowances — but paperwork matters.

Gifts and inheritance tax

Inheritance tax does not only apply on death. Certain gifts made during lifetime can also fall within the tax net.

Potentially Exempt Transfers (PETs)

Most lifetime gifts are classed as Potentially Exempt Transfers. These become fully exempt if the person making the gift survives seven years.

If death occurs within seven years

If the donor dies within seven years, some or all of the gift may become chargeable to inheritance tax.

The seven-year rule (explained simply)

The seven-year rule determines whether lifetime gifts become taxable.

  • Survive 7 years → gift usually tax-free
  • Death within 7 years → gift may be taxed
  • Taper relief may reduce tax after year 3

Importantly, taper relief reduces the tax, not the value of the gift itself.

Key point

The clock starts on the date of the gift — not the date it is reported.

Gift exemptions (often misunderstood)

Some gifts are immediately exempt from inheritance tax, regardless of timing.

Common exemptions

  • Annual exemption (small yearly allowance)
  • Small gifts to individuals
  • Normal expenditure out of income
  • Gifts to spouses or charities

Misusing or misunderstanding these exemptions is a frequent cause of unexpected inheritance tax bills.

Common inheritance tax traps

Inheritance tax problems often arise not from deliberate avoidance, but from assumptions and outdated advice.

  • “My house goes to my children automatically.”
    Ownership structure and wills matter.
  • “I gave it away years ago.”
    Gifts may still fall within the seven-year window.
  • “Spouses don’t pay inheritance tax.”
    True — but the tax may arise on the second death.
  • “Trusts avoid inheritance tax.”
    Trusts have their own complex tax regime.

Key point

Most inheritance tax issues arise from complexity, not intention.

Trusts and inheritance tax

Trusts are often mentioned in discussions about inheritance tax, but they are frequently misunderstood. Trusts do not automatically avoid inheritance tax and can introduce their own tax charges.

How trusts interact with inheritance tax

When assets are placed into trust, inheritance tax may arise immediately, later, or both — depending on the type of trust and the value involved.

  • Entry charges when assets are placed into trust
  • Periodic charges (usually every 10 years)
  • Exit charges when assets leave the trust

Key point

Trusts are planning tools — not shortcuts — and must be used carefully.

Common trust types (high-level)

Different trusts are taxed differently. The structure matters more than the name.

  • Discretionary trusts — most flexible, but subject to periodic and exit charges.
  • Interest in possession trusts — beneficiary has an immediate right to income.
  • Bare trusts — assets treated as belonging to the beneficiary.

Choosing the wrong trust structure can create tax issues rather than solve them.

Business Property Relief (BPR)

Business Property Relief can reduce the value of certain business assets for inheritance tax purposes — sometimes by up to 100%.

Assets that may qualify

  • Shares in qualifying trading companies
  • Business interests
  • Certain partnership assets

Investment businesses and passive activities usually do not qualify.

Key point

Whether a business qualifies depends on activity, not label.

Agricultural Property Relief (APR)

Agricultural Property Relief applies to qualifying agricultural property, including farmland and farm buildings, subject to strict conditions.

Key factors

  • Type of land and use
  • Length of ownership
  • Occupation and control

APR does not usually cover the full value of farmhouses or development value.

Key point

Agricultural relief is technical — small details make large differences.

Planning vs avoidance

There is an important legal and practical distinction between inheritance tax planning and tax avoidance.

Inheritance tax planning

  • Uses reliefs and exemptions provided by law
  • Requires documentation and timing
  • Is transparent and defensible

Tax avoidance schemes

  • Often marketed as “guaranteed”
  • Rely on artificial structures
  • May be challenged by HMRC

Key point

If something sounds risk-free and guaranteed, it usually isn’t.

Frequently asked questions

Does everyone pay inheritance tax?

No. Many estates fall below thresholds or qualify for exemptions and reliefs.

Is inheritance tax paid before probate?

Some inheritance tax is usually due before probate can be granted, although instalment options may apply for certain assets.

Can inheritance tax be paid in instalments?

Yes. HMRC allows instalments for certain assets, such as property, subject to interest.

Does having a will reduce inheritance tax?

A will does not reduce tax by itself, but it allows exemptions, reliefs and allowances to be used properly.

Final thoughts

Inheritance tax is not just a financial issue — it is a legal, emotional and administrative one.

Most problems arise not because people ignore inheritance tax, but because the rules are complex and change over time.

Key point

Understanding comes first. Decisions should follow — not the other way around.
